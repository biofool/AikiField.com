! ! group know that dialogue will follow the exercise to review the

In this manual, we follow the path of the spinal energy centers starting from
the bottom up - Sacrum, Belly, Solar Plexus, Heart, Throat, Brow and
Crown. In the Yogic tradition it is assumed that the path to health and
liberation is to open each center and allow the natural flow of energy up
from the earth and out the crown. Once this channel is open, one becomes
capable of receiving the energy of heaven - natural enlightenment.
To use a more western metaphor, Ohm’s law states that the amount of
energy passing through a conductor is dependent on the amount of energy
and the resistance in that conductor. Stress causes resistance in our
system - the fight, flight or freeze response. When it’s triggered, we
experience increased long muscle tension, increased heart and breath
rates, a racing mind and a narrowed field of perception. We literally clinch
our body and mind and lessen the flow of our energy. This reaction will
save our life on the freeway, but it’s killing us in the board room. These
exercises serve as an antidote to the fight or flight response, opening the
system for generative presence even under pressure.

> Scan your attention slowly around the body noticing where there’s felt/
sense [warmth, coolness, tingling. . .] and where there’s not. Don’t do
anything about the blank spots, just noticing will generate more felt/sense.
> Scan Neural Centers - Scan the three major energy centers of the
body [3 neural nexus / 3 brains]. Sense into the belly, the heart and the
head. Let go of any tension in or around each center. Notice any
differences. Which centers have felt/sense and which not ? Comfortable/
uncomfortable ?
> Sweep the attention up from the ground, through the legs, up the spine
through the belly, heart and head and sweep back down - notice any
differences ?
Centering the Body
> Plum Line - Demonstrate rocking left and right, less and less and less
until you align with your spine’s center line. Release any muscular tension
onto your skeletal frame and open the spaces between the vertebrae just
slightly. Feel. Do the same practice forward and back. Wobble into center
and release. Notice centerings effect. What sensations/feelings arise ?
Try just sending your attention to one side and then back. You might try an
Arm Grab/Sudden Change Test adding centering before and after the grab.

> Push Test - Select a volunteer and tell them what you’re going to do.
With their permission, push into their chest. Their job is to prevent you from
pushing them back - without moving their hands or feet. Now demonstrate
by standing next to them and pushing them on the upper chest. The first
time go in quickly to draw their counter move forward to match your force. If
they don’t move then repeat and push them back.
This usually draws the counter when you try a third time. Point out flight,
freeze or flight responses. Ask the individual and the group what they
observed - likely collapsing back or countering by leaning forward.
Now instruct them to focus their attention down through their feet into the
ground and ‘lightening rod’ the force of your push right down through their
body into the ground - imagine roots. Push again and demonstrate the
difference. Now ask the group to select partners and investigate these
responses with each other. The task of the partner pushing is not to push
them off center, but to rather to coach them to find their grounded center.

> The Crucible - This exercise provides an excellent live coaching
environment. Here it is used as a pre-post testing experience. Used before
teaching the principles and practices of Attention Mastery, it will reveal each
person’s go-to strategy/style when under the pressure of a complex,
multiple force situation. Used at the end of a session, it functions as an
evaluator and inculcator of what’s been learned as participants declare the
quality of leadership they want to demonstrate and step into the crucible to
test their commitment and new found skills before their peers.
The Crucible is a simulation of leadership and mindfulness under the
pressure of complex, unpredictable change. It’s a comparatively slow group
tag game that’s been adapted to test Leadership Presence under duress.
Tactically, the challenge is to calmly manage each given force and not to
©QE

QE - Facilitator’s Manual - Embodying Leadership Presence - 2016
get tagged, but this will only produce avoidance or over control.
Strategically, the challenge is to demonstrate gentle leadership of the other
players and even find a place to pause now and then. Finally contextually,
the challenge is to discover how to compassionately lead the group as a
whole system.
Ultimately, of course, the question is whether participants can let go of what
they know and innovate, responding intuitively and spontaneously in the
midst of the melee.
1. To help establish the relevance of the exercise, suggest that each
participant select a difficult situation or person from their professional or
personal lives that they will use for application inquiry.
2. Demonstrate with a group of four volunteers who will act as change
forces, asking them to surround you in an imaginary circle about 12 feet
across.
3. First demo the Force Role of the four people on the outside of the circle -
have a volunteer stand in the center as the Responder Role and clasping
your fists together and extending them out in front of you to form a spear,
walk toward them. Show that if they don’t get off the line, you’ll tag them
lightly - even if they duck in place. Then show and explain that if they get
out of the way [have them do so], the person in the Force Role continues
in a straight line to the other side of the imagined circle. No heat seeking
is allowed: that is, they can’t turn into the Responder as they go by or
chase them.
4. When they get to the other side of the circle, they turn around and take
new aim at the Responder wherever the Responder is at that moment.
The Responder’s new position becomes the new target line for the Force
player and they come again holding to that line across whatever large or
small cut of the circle that requires. Explain that those playing the Force
Role will all be doing this simultaneously, and even if they bump into
each other [and they will], they are to resume their line and continue
once the traffic jam has cleared.
©QE

5. Ask for questions.
6. Now demonstrate the Responder Role. Step into the center and let them
know that the Responder gets to control when the action begins and
ends and the speed of the other Force Players. Invite them to come for
you and demo moving smoothly amongst them. If the exercise is being
used to reveal reactivity styles, don’t demonstrate the full range of
engagement options. If you’re demonstrating for a coaching or evaluation
round, then show not only facile movement but light physical guidance
for redirection, the use of that guidance to engage the Force players with
each other [using one to block another], finding the open space for pause
and even aligning the force players such that they are affected as a
whole system.
7. Ask the group to select partners in groups of 5 and investigate.
Emphasize safety first. the most common problem is when someone
from one group steps back onto the foot or ankle of someone in another
group. Establish a Safety Stop Signal [whistle, clap or shout] that will be
used to stop all action if needed. Warn them to avoid faces, glasses and
women’s chests.
8. After each player tries a round in the center of 30 seconds or so, the
group should give the Responder feedback - first on how well their
behavior/style/strategy worked and second on what didn’t work about
their approach. Then the next responder has their turn and so on until
everyone in the group has played.
9. Once done the group should dialogue about what happened, what they
learned about their style of reactivity and how their play relates to how
they react to pressure in their professional or personal lives.

> The Crucible - Used at the end of a session, The Crucible functions as
an evaluator and inculcator of what’s been learned as participants declare
the quality of leadership they want to demonstrate and step into the
crucible to test their commitment and new found skills before their peers. W
The Crucible is a simulation of leadership and mindfulness under the
pressure of unpredictable change. Tactically, the challenge is to manage
each given force and not to get tagged, but this will only produce avoidance
or over control. Strategically, the challenge is to gently lead the other
players with compassion and even find a place to pause now and then.
Finally contextually, the challenge is to discover how to lead the group as a
whole system.
Ultimately, of course, the question is whether participants can let go of what
they know and innovate, responding intuitively and spontaneously in the
midst of the melee. To help establish the relevance of the exercise, suggest
that each participant select a difficult situation or person from their
professional or personal lives that they will use for application inquiry.
Set Up - Again emphasize safety first. the most common problem is when
someone from one group steps back onto the foot or ankle of someone in
another group. Establish a Safety Stop Signal [whistle, clap or shout] that
will be used to stop all action if needed. Warn them to avoid faces, glasses
and women’s chests.
Once done the group should dialogue about what happened, what they
learned and how this exercise relates to their professional or personal
lives. The facilitator may choose to intervene in a given group to coach the
