Security has become a business problem

You may not need a permanent CISO.

But you may need someone who can take responsibility for security now.

Perhaps a major customer has sent a security questionnaire that your engineers do not have time to complete. Perhaps a sales opportunity is waiting on answers your team cannot confidently provide. Perhaps cyber-insurance requirements have exposed gaps that nobody has the capacity to organize.

Or perhaps your CTO and lead engineers are doing a reasonable job — and you see that security is limiting your ability to deliver features.

Sure, your CTO and lead engineers can handle security. Is that really what you hired them for? Is that what they are passionate about?

Want to keep them focused on their core skill set? Maybe a fractional CISO is what you are looking for.

  • Can we satisfy this customer's security requirements?
  • What actually needs to be fixed first?
  • Who owns the security program?
  • How much will this require from engineering?
  • Do we need a full-time CISO — or practical leadership for the next stage?

CISO-level leadership without a full-time hire

A Fractional CISO gives your company senior security leadership at the point when you need judgment, coordination, and execution—but before a full-time security executive is justified. The engagement is built around the business you are actually running, not a generic enterprise checklist. That means understanding: What your product does. How your company makes money. Which customers matter most. What your customers and insurers actually require. Where your engineering team is already strong. Where risk is concentrated. Which improvements will reduce the most exposure with the least disruption. The goal is not to buy every security tool or promise perfect security. The goal is to build the level of security your business needs to win trust, protect customers, support growth, and make informed decisions.

A practical path from uncertainty to maintenance

Every engagement is tailored to the company's stage, customers, and risk. A typical pattern looks like this.

1

First 30 days: Understand the field

AikiField interviews key stakeholders one-on-one and listens across the company—not only to the people who already own security. That may include leadership, engineering, QA, product, infrastructure, operations, Sales, and customer-facing teams. Selected engineering meetings and weekly leadership meetings help reveal how security work actually happens, where decisions get stuck, and which concerns are material. By the end of this phase, you should have: A shared view of the current situation. A first picture of the most important risks. An understanding of customer and insurance expectations. A draft plan with an initial estimate of effort and ownership.

2

First 60 days: Turn priorities into action

The draft plan becomes a practical implementation list. Together, we identify: The technology and vendors that are genuinely needed. The processes that need to change. The evidence customers may require. The vulnerabilities and control gaps that matter most. The work engineering, leadership, and external partners must own. What can wait. This is where security stops being a collection of concerns and becomes an ordered set of decisions.

3

By 90 days: Move toward maintenance mode

The objective is not to create permanent dependence on a consultant. The objective is to establish a security program your team can operate. By this point, the company should have a clearer roadmap, defined ownership, an operating rhythm, and a practical way to respond when a questionnaire arrives or a customer security team requests a conversation. AikiField can then remain available for ongoing leadership, periodic review, customer assurance, significant decisions, and the situations where experienced judgment matters most.

Security that helps close the deal

Security work should protect revenue—not quietly prevent it. In previous work, Kenneth Kron recovered access to a blocked sales channel representing $5 million in annual recurring revenue by resolving the security objections holding multiple customers back. He also rebuilt a vulnerability workflow that reduced the average time from identification and escalation to remediation from 165 days to 21 days, then helped reduce average mitigation time further to 3 days through AI-assisted triage and remediation workflows. Those outcomes did not come from adding complexity for its own sake. They came from understanding what customers needed, identifying the real constraints, and focusing effort where it changed the business result.

Make security questionnaires boring

A security questionnaire should not become an emergency every time a prospect sends one. A practical security program gives your team:

  • Reusable, accurate answers.
  • Clear evidence ownership.
  • A process for handling exceptions.
  • A consistent way to describe the roadmap.
  • Someone who can join the customer conversation.
  • Confidence that the answers reflect how the product actually works.

The same principle applies to penetration tests and external audits. The aim is to make them predictable, manageable, and useful—not mysterious events that interrupt the entire company.

Frequently asked questions

Is a Fractional CISO only for companies preparing for SOC 2?

No. Compliance may be one trigger, but the need often begins with an enterprise customer, insurance requirement, vulnerability backlog, product risk, or an executive team that needs clearer ownership.

Will AikiField replace our CTO or engineering team?

No. AikiField provides leadership, prioritization, coordination, and specialist judgment. The aim is to help your existing team work more effectively without asking them to carry an additional executive responsibility indefinitely.

Do we need to hire a full-time CISO eventually?

Possibly. The right answer depends on the company's size, risk, customer requirements, security workload, and growth. A Fractional CISO can help you make that decision from evidence rather than urgency.

Is this a compliance-only engagement?

No. Compliance is one part of the picture. The work is connected to customers, revenue, product risk, engineering capacity, resilience, and business priorities.
