Aikido and Conflict Resolution

what the mat teaches about the moment before things escalate

By Richard Moon · Martial Arts

Every conflict has a moment before it becomes a conflict.
A word. A tone. A grab.
What happens in that moment decides everything after it.

Aikido is sometimes described as a martial art with no offense — every technique is a response to an attack that's already been initiated. That single design choice turns out to contain most of what I know about resolving conflict between people, whether the "attack" is a fist, a raised voice, or an accusatory email.

None of this is theory built after the fact. It's what three decades of teaching Aikido, and then bringing it into boardrooms, mediation rooms, and post-conflict zones, actually showed me works.

get off the line first

The first Aikido response to an attack is not a block. It's a step. You move off the line of the attack — out of its direct path — so the force that was aimed at you no longer has you as its target. From that position, facing the same direction as your training partner, you can see what they're actually doing instead of just feeling the impact of it.

Conflict between people works the same way. The instinct when criticized or attacked verbally is to stand your ground and meet force with force — block, counter, defend the position. Getting off the line means something different: not collapsing, not disappearing, but repositioning so the accusation has room to land without you having to absorb it, argue with it, or return it. From that angle, you can actually see what the other person is defending, wanting, or afraid of — which is usually not what their words are saying on the surface.

You are not required to stand in front of an attack to respond to it well.

Moving is not surrender. It's how you get a clear view of what's actually happening.

find out what's really going on

Once you're off the line, the next question is WIGO — What Is Really Going On. Almost no conflict is actually about its stated subject. The argument about whose turn it is to do the dishes is rarely about dishes. The negotiation stalled over one contract clause is rarely about that clause. Beneath the surface position is a need, a fear, or a history that the words are only pointing at.

You can't see that layer while you're reacting. That's why the practice starts with noticing your own state before diagnosing anyone else's. A useful tool here is the Def-Con Scale — naming, even just to yourself, how activated you currently are, from calm and open down toward full escalation. The naming itself creates a half-second of distance. That half-second is often the entire difference between a conversation that stays open and one that closes.

listen to the reactivity, not just the words

Reactivity — the heat, the defensiveness, the urge to interrupt — isn't a malfunction in a difficult conversation. It's information. It tells you what the other person cares about, because reactivity only shows up around things that matter. Extraordinary Listening means receiving that heat as part of the message instead of either suppressing your own or reacting to theirs.

This is where most conflict resolution advice stops short. "Stay calm" and "don't take it personally" are true but incomplete — they tell you what not to do without saying what to do instead. The instead is: listen to the reactivity itself as content. What is this anger actually protecting? What does this person need to feel heard before anything else can move?

Turning an Indictment into a Request

Hear the accusation.
"You never listen to me." "This team doesn't respect deadlines." Whatever the indictment is, don't argue with it yet.

Ask what unmet need is underneath it.
An indictment is almost always a badly delivered request. "You never listen to me" often means "I need to know what I say to you matters."

Respond to the need, not the accusation.
You can't win an argument about whether you "never" listen. You can respond to a need for being taken seriously. That's a conversation that can actually move somewhere.

This technique — turning indictments into requests — comes directly out of the listening work, and it draws on Marshall Rosenberg's Nonviolent Communication. It's one of the simplest tools I teach, and one of the most consistently underused, because in the heat of a conflict it's much easier to defend against an indictment than to hear the request buried inside it.

everyone needs to be counted and heard

A great deal of conflict — in a marriage, a company, or a country — comes down to two needs I call the Tribal Roster and the Tribal Council. The roster is being counted: knowing your welfare is considered, that you belong to the group. The council is having a voice: knowing your input actually shapes what the group decides. Deny either one long enough, and you get frustration, then apathy, then — in extreme cases — violence.

Most conflict resolution that fails, fails because it addresses the stated dispute while leaving one of these two needs unmet. You can settle the terms of an agreement perfectly and still leave people feeling uncounted or unheard, and the conflict will resurface somewhere else. Real resolution checks both: does everyone feel like they belong here, and does everyone feel like they were actually listened to?

center, blend, lead

On the mat, the sequence is always the same: center yourself, blend with the energy coming at you, then lead it somewhere useful. Off the mat, in a business or a negotiation, the same three moves apply — the Three Easy Lessons in their working-life form. Centering means staying steady enough to think clearly under pressure. Blending means harmonizing with what's actually happening instead of opposing it head-on — power with the situation rather than power against it. Leading means that once you're centered and aligned, you can actually direct where the energy goes, rather than just surviving the encounter.

Some of the deepest tests of this sequence I've been part of weren't in a dojo or a boardroom at all — they were in rooms where people had been on opposite sides of real, sometimes violent, history. I've written separately about that work in Cyprus and in Bosnia. The scale is different from a workplace disagreement, but the underlying moves — get off the line, find out what's really going on, listen to the reactivity, make sure everyone is counted and heard — are the same ones.

the pause is where the choice lives

None of this works without a gap between the trigger and the response. I call that gap the Quantum Pause — the interval where a reaction hasn't yet hardened into an action, where there's still room to choose center over reactivity. Conflict resolution isn't a script you follow once things have already escalated. It's a set of moves available in that pause, before escalation locks in.

You don't have to win the argument.
You have to find out what's really going on, hear what's underneath it, and make sure everyone in the room is counted.

That's not a compromise. That's the practice.
