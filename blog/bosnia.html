bosnia

reconciliation after war

Peace Work  ·  Mid-1990s

Cyprus Bosnia Herzegovina Pacific Rim
Cyprus was about positions.
Bosnia was about wounds.
The work is different when the war
just ended.

I went to Bosnia in the mid-1990s, not long after the Dayton Accords brought the fighting to a stop. The ceasefire was recent enough that nobody quite believed it yet. The physical destruction was visible everywhere. And the human destruction — what happens to a community that has lived through what Bosnia lived through — that was visible in ways harder to describe.

This was different from Cyprus.

In Cyprus, the wound was old. Thirty years of division had converted acute pain into something more like chronic numbness. The communities were separated, estranged, but not still bleeding. The work there was to thaw something frozen.

In Bosnia, the wound was fresh. The work there was something else entirely.

the context

The war in Bosnia — 1992 to 1995, was the bloodiest European conflict since World War II. Roughly 100,000 people killed. More than two million displaced. The deliberate destruction of communities, of cultural memory, of the idea that neighbors could also be fellow citizens. Sarajevo had been under siege for nearly four years: the longest siege of a capital city in modern warfare.

By the time I arrived, the Dayton Accords had ended the fighting and divided the country into entities. But political arrangements don't heal people. They just create the conditions — sometimes: in which healing might eventually become possible.

Our work was part of that longer, slower process.

the harder problem

In a frozen conflict like Cyprus, the challenge is to create enough heat, enough contact, enough honesty: to begin thawing positions that have calcified over decades. The emotions are there, but they're cold. Defended. The work is to create safety for them to surface.

In a post-war situation like Bosnia, the challenge is almost the opposite. The emotions are not frozen. They're raw. Immediate. The grief is not abstract. The anger is not theoretical. People have specific losses. Specific faces. Specific moments they cannot stop returning to.

You cannot negotiate your way past that. You cannot use technique to process it. The Harvard frameworks, brilliant for certain kinds of conflict: are not designed for the moment when someone across the table from you lost their child six months ago.

For that, something else is required.

what the mat teaches about grief

Aikido has a principle that translates roughly as "entering." When someone attacks, you don't step back: you move toward them, into the line of attack, and from that position redirect the energy. You enter the situation rather than avoiding it.

Working with raw grief requires something similar. You don't manage it. You don't redirect it prematurely. You enter it. You sit with it. You let it be what it is without rushing to resolve it or make it productive.

This sounds obvious. It is extremely difficult in practice.

Every instinct in a facilitation context pushes toward resolution. Toward progress. Toward the next step in the process. Silence feels like failure. Pain feels like something to fix. The urge to intervene, to offer something, to redirect, to move things along: is almost overwhelming.

Staying present with what's actually happening, without moving to manage it: that is the practice. That is what the mat trains, over years, in body and nervous system. Receive. Don't flinch. Don't fix. Let the energy complete itself.

You cannot reconcile people
who haven't yet been heard.
The first act of peace is witness.

what reconciliation actually means

The word gets used too loosely. People use it to mean forgiveness, or agreement, or forgetting. None of those are what it means. None of those can be achieved on anyone's timetable but the person who has to do it.

What reconciliation actually means, in the sense that our work pointed toward: is something more modest and more fundamental: the ability to share a world with someone you have reason to hate. Not to stop having the reason. Not to stop feeling whatever you feel. But to be in the same room. To conduct a meeting. To negotiate a practical arrangement. To let the other person exist without it consuming you.

That is not a small thing. After what happened in Bosnia, that was an extraordinary thing.

And it requires, as its foundation, acknowledgment. Someone has to say: I know what happened to you. I know what my side did. I know it was wrong. Not as a diplomatic formula. As a human being to a human being.

The Harvard Negotiation Project's framework distinguishes between positions, interests, and needs. In Bosnia, the deepest need was almost always acknowledgment. Not land. Not rights, although those matter. Acknowledgment. Witness. The recognition that what happened was real, and wrong, and matters.

When that acknowledgment was genuinely offered, not performed, not tactical, but actually felt: something in the room shifted. Not resolution. But movement. The first, fragile possibility of something other than permanent enmity.

what doesn't work

Process doesn't work when the people running it aren't actually present. I've been in facilitation sessions where the facilitator was running their method rather than attending to the room. You can feel the difference. The participants feel it too, even when they can't name it.

Technique doesn't work when what's needed is witness. There's a time for reframing, for interest-based negotiation, for structured dialogue. And there's a time when someone needs to be heard, completely, without the facilitator moving to the next step on the agenda. Learning to distinguish these moments, and to be comfortable staying in the harder one: that's the practice.

Premature reconciliation doesn't work. If you push people toward reconciliation before they're ready, before they feel heard, before the grief has had some room: you create a brittle agreement that fractures under any pressure. Real reconciliation is slow. It cannot be accelerated by good intentions or tight timelines.

The sword that cuts things together, not apart.
Aiki is not quick.
Harmony is not convenient.

the connection to Quantum Aikido

The work in Bosnia deepened something I had already begun to understand in Cyprus: that what I had spent twenty years learning on the mat was not separate from this work. It was the preparation for it.

O Sensei's teaching, that Aikido is about harmony, about transforming conflict rather than defeating it: is easy to understand as philosophy. It's harder to embody. Years of physical practice train the body to not flinch, to receive, to stay centered under pressure. That embodied quality transfers. When you are physically incapable of panic, you create different conditions in a room than when you're managing your own anxiety while trying to manage others'.

The Quantum Pause, the interval between stimulus and response: is not just a concept. In Bosnia, it was a practice I returned to constantly. Something devastating would be said. Something that would, in any normal social situation, demand immediate response. And the practice was to pause. Not to suppress. Not to avoid. But to pause long enough to feel what was actually happening before deciding what, if anything, to do.

That pause is what creates space for something other than reaction to occur.

what stayed with me

Bosnia taught me something I had not fully understood before: that the capacity for harm is not located in particular nations or ethnicities or religions. It's located in human beings under certain conditions: conditions of fear, of perceived existential threat, of propaganda and tribalism and the slow erosion of the other's humanity.

And the capacity for reconciliation, the capacity to turn toward someone you have reason to hate and find the human being there: that is also located in human beings. Under different conditions. Conditions of safety, of witness, of time, of someone present enough to hold the space while something that takes a long time unfolds.

I left Bosnia with no certainty about outcomes. The political situation remained deeply complicated. The communities faced long, difficult work ahead. What I had witnessed were moments, small, real, significant moments: of people finding each other across an abyss.

That's what's possible. That's what the work is for.
