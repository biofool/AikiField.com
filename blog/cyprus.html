cyprus

peace across the green line

Peace Work  ·  Mid-1990s

Cyprus Bosnia Herzegovina Pacific Rim
Two peoples.
One island.
Decades of silence across a line no one drew cleanly.
Our job: sit in the same room and not destroy each other.

I arrived in Cyprus in the mid-1990s not as a tourist and not as a diplomat. I came as part of a team attempting something that official channels had failed to do for thirty years: create genuine dialogue between Greek Cypriot and Turkish Cypriot community leaders.

This is called track-two diplomacy. Not politicians bound by official positions. Not peace treaties negotiated behind closed doors. Community leaders — educators, business people, civic figures — people with enough influence to shift their communities' thinking, but without the institutional constraints that make official dialogue impossible.

The theory is elegant. The practice is not.

the island, divided

Cyprus has been effectively partitioned since 1974, when Turkish military intervention followed a Greek-sponsored coup. The Green Line — a UN buffer zone cutting across Nicosia: divided the island into two communities who had lived together, then couldn't.

By the time our team arrived, twenty years of separation had done their work. Not just political separation. Emotional separation. The kind where the other side stops being people and becomes a concept, a threat, a symbol of what was lost. You can negotiate with governments. You cannot negotiate with a symbol.

Our framework, developed by the Institute for Multi-Track Diplomacy, recognized this. Before positions can shift, people have to meet. Before agreements can hold, understanding has to exist. This work was conducted in association with the Fulbright Commission and the American Embassy in Cyprus — and with Douglas Stone of the Harvard Negotiation Project, who later wrote the foreword to Quantum Aikido.

Partnership Framework

  • Institute for Multi-Track Diplomacy: founded by Louise Diamond and Ambassador John W. McDonald; conveners of the dialogue
  • Harvard Negotiation Project: analytical framework; Douglas Stone, co-author of Difficult Conversations
  • Fulbright Commission / American Embassy in Cyprus: institutional support and access

the first night

We brought twenty Greek Cypriots and twenty Turkish Cypriots together. That alone was extraordinary. These communities had not been in the same room for years, some of them for decades.

I remember the first night clearly: not the session itself, but the meeting beforehand. Five facilitators. Hours of planning. Nobody agreeing. Everyone exhausted. The energy was wrong. Tight. Contracted. The kind of group mind that couldn't see past its own fatigue.

I said something like: "If you look at the totality, what's wrong points to what's right. Envision tomorrow if it were great."

Then I laughed. "Of course, I could be wrong. Anything can happen."

The room changed.

That moment taught me something I already knew from thirty years on the mat, but had never seen demonstrated so cleanly in a political context: state is contagious. The quality of the facilitators' presence would determine the quality of the space available to the participants. If we were contracted, we would create contracted space. If we were open, something else became possible.

what track-two actually is

Track-one diplomacy is official negotiations. Treaties. Summits. Ministers with prepared statements and carefully worded communiqués.

Track-two is something else. It's the conversation that has to happen before the official conversation can happen. The one where people stop representing their governments and start speaking as human beings.

This is much harder than it sounds. People come in wearing their positions like armor. Every word is parsed for advantage. Every concession feels like defeat. The first hours, sometimes the first days: can feel completely stuck. Two groups in a room, technically talking, but no real contact occurring.

In Aikido terms, they're pushing against each other. Meeting force with force. Going nowhere.

the turn

The shift never comes from argument. I have never once seen someone argued into genuine understanding.

It comes from a moment of actual contact. Someone stops defending their position long enough to feel something. Or asks a real question: not a rhetorical question designed to score a point, but a genuine question. "What was that like for you?" "What did your family lose?" "What are you most afraid of?"

When those questions get asked, and answered honestly: the dynamic changes. You can feel it in the room. The temperature shifts. Bodies that were rigid start to soften. Not agreement. Not resolution. Something more fundamental: recognition. One human being recognizing another.

You cannot make peace with a symbol.
You can only make peace with a person.
Everything before that moment is preparation.

In Cyprus, I watched a Turkish Cypriot man pause, genuinely pause: while a Greek Cypriot described what his family had experienced in 1974. Something in the quality of the pause was different. Not tactical. Not polite. Actually absorbing what was being said.

He asked a question. The Greek Cypriot stopped. Looked at him. And answered differently than he had ever answered before. Because someone had actually asked.

That's the whole practice. Create enough space. Hold it steadily. Let the human beings find each other.

what the mat taught the table

I had been practicing Aikido for more than twenty years when I went to Cyprus. On the surface, these seem like unrelated domains. A Japanese martial art. A Mediterranean peace process.

But the principles are identical.

In Aikido, you learn not to meet force with force. You learn to receive incoming energy, blend with it, and redirect it. You learn to feel what's actually happening, not what you expect, not what you fear, but what's actually there. You learn that your own state, contracted or open, reactive or centered: determines what becomes possible.

A negotiation is not different. Every charged statement is incoming energy. Every moment of defensiveness is a clash, an impasse, energy blocked. The facilitator's job is the same as the Aikidoka's: stay centered, receive the energy, don't resist: and create the conditions for it to transform.

The mat is a laboratory for conflict.
What works there works everywhere.
The principles don't change. The stakes do.

I wasn't applying a technique in Cyprus. I was being what I had trained to be. There's a difference. Techniques run out. Training becomes character.

the quantum dimension

When Joseph S.A. Bois introduced the concept of WIGO, What Is Going On: he meant the totality of a situation, always exceeding any single observer's perception. No one in that room in Cyprus had the whole picture. Not the Greek Cypriots. Not the Turkish Cypriots. Not the facilitators.

The practice was to sense beneath surface appearances before acting. To ask, not rhetorically, but as genuine inquiry: what is really happening here? What's the energy beneath the stated position? What's the need beneath the demand? What's the fear beneath the anger?

In quantum mechanics, the act of observation affects what's observed. The quality of your attention shapes what becomes visible. This is as true in a room of Cypriots as in a particle accelerator.

When facilitators were fully present, not managing the process, not thinking about the next intervention, but actually present to the human beings in the room: participants felt it. And responded to it. The quality of attention created the quality of space.

That's the observer effect. And in thirty years of working with groups, I have never found a more reliable principle.

what didn't happen

We did not solve the Cyprus problem. The Green Line still exists. The political situation remains unresolved. If you judge track-two diplomacy by whether it produces treaties, you will always be disappointed.

But something happened that matters. Twenty people from each side of a decades-long divide sat together, dropped their positions for long enough to be human with each other, and found: not agreement, but recognition. Some of those relationships continued. Some of those people went back to their communities and spoke differently. Some of them changed.

Real change is slow. It moves from person to person, conversation to conversation. It doesn't look like a summit. It doesn't generate headlines. It's more like water: working its way through, finding cracks, gradually shifting what seems immovable.

What was confirmed for me in Cyprus was this: the principles I'd been practicing on the mat for twenty years were exactly the right preparation for this work. Not despite being a martial artist. Because of it. O Sensei called Aikido "the realization of love." In Cyprus, I understood what he meant.

Douglas Stone

One of the people I met in Cyprus was Douglas Stone of the Harvard Negotiation Project: co-author of Difficult Conversations, one of the most precise books ever written about what happens in hard exchanges between human beings. We recognized each other immediately. Different vocabularies, same understanding.

Thirty years later, Douglas wrote the foreword to Quantum Aikido: The Power of Harmony. He understood instantly what happens when Aikido principles enter negotiation work: because he'd been in the room when it happened.

That's standing on the floating bridge of heaven.
That's where 1 + 1 = 1.
