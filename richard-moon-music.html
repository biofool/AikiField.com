Richard Moon

Two practices, one thread: improvisation

Richard Moon began as a musician before he found yoga and Aikido. Improvisation connects the music, the practice and the way he teaches.

Opening

Ask Richard Moon how he came to Aikido and he starts with music. Before yoga, and before the dojo, he was a musician. Decades later, the same instinct runs through how he teaches. In his foreword to Quantum Aikido: The Power of Harmony, Douglas Stone describes Richard as a musician who plays in many styles and lives as an improviser. This page follows that thread from the music to the dojo.

Music first

Richard's path ran from music to yoga to Aikido, and each step kept something from the one before it. In his own account, music came first.

That is a useful background for someone who later became a teacher of an art that depends on responding to another person in real time. It also helps explain why Richard has little patience for technique that is performed without attention to what is happening in the room.

The word improviser is easy to misread. It does not mean someone who makes things up at random. It means someone who has practiced enough to respond to what is actually happening, and who trusts that preparation to show up when the moment calls for it.

Aikido as improvisation

Richard came to yoga and then to Aikido after music, and he describes Aikido in his own words as a way of life more than a way of fighting. When he teaches, the emphasis falls on listening: to the person in front of you, to the situation, and to your own state. Techniques matter, but they are what comes out when attention is clear. They are not the starting point.

An improvising musician knows the same thing. You can practice scales for years, and the scales matter, but the music happens when you stop thinking about them and start listening. Aikido, as Richard teaches it, asks the student to find the same place under pressure, with a partner who is moving toward them.

The aiki-dance form

Richard has developed an improvisational form that fuses Aikido movement with music. It brings the two practices together in a single activity. The movement comes from Aikido, and the music sets the context in which the movement is improvised.

Because the form is improvised, it shows quickly whether the participants are listening to one another. A partner who tries to control the dance loses the music, and a partner who only follows loses the dance. The skill is to blend with what is happening and lead gently where it is wanted. That is the same skill Richard teaches for conversations, negotiations and conflict, practiced in a setting where it is easier to see and easier to enjoy.

Why it matters for leaders

People who come to Richard through his executive coaching sometimes find the musical side surprising. A leader in a stuck meeting faces the same problem as a player in a stuck song, which is how to listen well enough to find the next phrase. The practice of centering, blending and leading that Richard teaches in organizations is the same attention he brings to music, described in different words.

Writing and recordings

Richard's teaching, including talks and conversations on listening, appears on the Moonsensei channel on YouTube and on the Ki to the City podcast. His book Quantum Aikido: The Power of Harmony, published by Park Street Press in January 2026, puts his thinking about attention, harmony and response in writing. You can read more about him on his page.

For Richard the two practices do not compete. Music trains the ear and Aikido trains the body, and both train a person to stay open while something unpredictable is happening.

He often closes his teaching with a line that carries the whole approach: Don't get mad. Don't even get even. Create a beautiful world.
